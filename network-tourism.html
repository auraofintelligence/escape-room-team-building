ROUND ONE · 03 · Network and tourism

A tourism network that rewards return visits

Explore how distinctive local rooms, linked travel and renewed experiences could support repeat visits across Australia.

The network product

A connected visitor journey is one way to develop the national network aim. A room could remain a complete local experience while also giving visitors a reason to try another destination. Distinctive stories, task structures and local connections would be important to the value of the journey.

Formats to compare include a local day with linked activities, a regional weekend with rooms and accommodation, and a passport used across a longer journey. Employer retreats and school series could add different forms of reflection and application. These are product concepts to research with their intended buyers.

Direct referrals, separately booked linked services and a package sold by a contracting operator create different support, pricing and settlement requirements. Comparing them does not establish partner participation or choose a selling structure.

Brisbane, Redlands and Minjerribah provide one route concept for development. Sunshine Coast and hinterland, Northern Rivers and Coffs Harbour, and Melbourne, Beechworth and Albury provide other possible regional patterns. These are development examples. Actual participating venues, ferry or road timetables, accessible transport, cultural hosts and accommodation must be assembled for each published itinerary.

Travel itself creates useful practice: agreeing a meeting time, accommodating different needs, asking for assistance, changing a plan and sharing responsibility. The next room introduces a fresh task where people can use their skills without an in-game reminder.

The direct network pass and settlement

Worked illustration | 29 October 2026 | Quantities and business inputs are assumptions for comparison.

Worked illustration, 29 October 2026: a three-room pass at $169 per person, with $53 allocated to each venue redemption and $10 to a network service. The prices and allocations are inherited model assumptions. They are not competitor facts, negotiated terms or a selected tariff.

Against three assumed $59 admissions, the arithmetic difference is $8. For five passes in one transaction, receipts would be $845, comprising $795 for venue admissions and $50 for the network service before processing and delivery costs. The finance chapter shows why the amount collected and the network's own revenue must be distinguished.

Separate bookings, referral payments and other pass structures remain alternatives. A useful comparison would examine redemption patterns, customer value, provider costs, cancellations and distribution fees. Actual accounting treatment would depend on the contracting relationship and current professional advice.

How neighbouring businesses benefit

The commercial hypothesis is that linked activities could support an extra night or a planned meal break. These benefits would need to be measured through actual bookings. Galleries, museums, guides and cultural hosts can offer their own experiences between rooms. Local makers can supply set dressing, props, repairs and original stories. Councils and tourism organisations can help connect venues with precincts, visitor information and relevant regional programmes.

Measure partner benefit through authorised referral codes, actual redeemed bookings and optional group feedback. Report partner sales separately from the escape-room business's revenue. A useful local-impact report includes supplier payments, paid work, room admissions, attributed partner transactions and funded access places.

A participant-directed Aura connection could retain a chosen learning thread. A booking design would need to limit tourism partners to the information required for their service. Personal reflections are not a condition of cross-venue ticket redemption.

Rotation makes the network more valuable

Mystery Rooms provides a practical precedent for game movement: its current Geelong page describes consolidation at Fitzroy and long-term residencies for two games with operators in Beechworth and Albury. Its vouchers exclude those partner venues, demonstrating why a shared pass needs deliberately negotiated redemption terms.

Documented interfaces, transportable components and a version history are design options to examine for future rooms. A room can gain a new audience in another region while its previous venue receives a fresh experience. A rotation cost comparison would account for deinstallation, condition checks, freight, receiving-site works, commissioning, training and downtime. The venue and network each earn only the revenue their agreement provides.
